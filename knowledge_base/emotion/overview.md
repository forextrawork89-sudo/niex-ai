# Human Emotion Intelligence Architecture Framework

This document is the authoritative emotional foundation for the emotion subsystem.
It presents an encyclopedia-level framework for understanding human emotion as a layered phenomenon shaped by affective processes, appraisal, cognition, social interaction, culture, embodiment, time, and context.
The framework is conceptual and implementation-neutral. It avoids runtime architecture, memory management, WebAssembly references, ONNX references, GPU optimization, threading models, and execution pipelines.
Its purpose is to explain how emotions emerge, how they evolve, how they interact with cognition and context, how they influence interpretation, and how they should be modeled in future multimodal systems.

Primary commitments:
* Emotion is understood as a dynamic, situated, and socially mediated process rather than a simple label.
* Emotion reasoning integrates facial behavior, gaze, posture, gesture, movement, context, language, and social expectation.
* The framework supports explainable multimodal reasoning instead of reductive risk detection.
* Emotion analysis must preserve uncertainty, context, culture, and temporal change.

## 1. Human Emotion Foundations

Human emotion emerges from the coordination of physiological arousal, appraisal, memory, social expectation, and context-dependent interpretation.
An emotion is not a single observable signal. It is a structured pattern that includes felt state, expressive behavior, bodily change, cognitive evaluation, and social meaning.
The foundations of emotion intelligence lie in recognizing that emotion is both personal and relational and that its meaning depends on what is happening, who is present, what the person believes, and what the culture allows.

### 1.1 Affective Computing

Affective computing studies how computational systems reason about emotion, affective state, and expressive behavior.
The field is concerned not only with classification but also with how emotion shapes interpretation, social coherence, decision-making, and human-computer interaction.
Affective computing should preserve emotion as a distributed, temporally evolving construct rather than as a fixed category.

### 1.2 Appraisal Theory

Appraisal theory explains that emotions are shaped by how an event is evaluated in relation to goals, norms, agency, predictability, control, and accountability.
The same event can produce different emotional outcomes depending on the person’s interpretation of consequences, responsibility, and social meaning.
Appraisal theory is central for understanding why similar observable signals can carry different emotional interpretations.

### 1.3 Basic Emotion Theory

Basic emotion theory proposes that certain emotions form a set of universally recognizable affective categories such as joy, sadness, fear, anger, disgust, and surprise.
This framework treats basic emotions as useful conceptual anchors while recognizing that actual emotional life is richer, more contextual, and more graded.
Basic emotion categories provide a foundation for cross-situational reasoning but should not be treated as exhaustive or purely universal.

* 1.3.1: A basic-emotion case showing how a core affective category appears in a realistic human interaction.

### 1.4 Dimensional Emotion Theory

Dimensional emotion theory models affect in continuous space rather than in discrete labels.
The central axes of valence, arousal, and dominance help explain how emotional states vary in intensity, pleasantness, and control.
Dimensional models are well suited to tracking emotional trajectories and transitions across time.

* 1.4.1: A dimensional-emotion case showing how valence, arousal, and dominance describe a changing emotional state.
* 1.4.2: A dimensional-emotion case showing how valence, arousal, and dominance describe a changing emotional state.

### 1.5 Constructivist Emotion Theory

Constructivist emotion theory argues that emotions are shaped by language, memory, social learning, and cultural interpretation.
Emotions are not merely biologically hardwired but actively assembled from embodied experience and interpretation.
This theory is essential for explaining how the same expressive pattern can be interpreted differently across contexts and communities.

* 1.5.1: A constructivist-emotion case showing how cultural and situational meaning shape emotional interpretation.
* 1.5.2: A constructivist-emotion case showing how cultural and situational meaning shape emotional interpretation.

### 1.6 Social Emotion Theory

Social emotion theory studies emotions that arise from relationships, social evaluation, norms, reputation, and interdependence.
Empathy, admiration, embarrassment, shame, affection, jealousy, and politeness are examples of emotions that depend on social positioning and shared norms.
Social emotion theory is crucial because many emotionally meaningful moments are relational rather than individual.

## 3. Multimodal Emotion Framework

Emotion is expressed and inferred through multiple coordinated channels including facial expressions, gaze, posture, gesture, movement, context, and text-image correlation.
No single channel should determine emotion in isolation, because meaning emerges from the alignment and tension between channels.
The framework treats multimodal emotion reasoning as a structured integration problem rather than a simple feature fusion exercise.
