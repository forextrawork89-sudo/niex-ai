# 1. Purpose and Architectural Role

This document is the authoritative foundation for interaction reasoning throughout the intent subsystem.
It is not a runtime implementation guide, browser optimization note, or execution-performance specification.
It is a research-grade architecture for explaining how human interactions emerge, how interaction differs from individual behavior, and how local exchanges evolve into shared activities and shared intent.

### 1.1 Primary Purpose
The primary purpose of this framework is to explain how interactions are perceived, interpreted, and structured from observable behavior.
The framework treats interaction as a relational phenomenon, not merely as a collection of nearby bodies or synchronous movements.
It explains how social meaning is constructed through communication, role alignment, shared attention, coordinated actions, and temporal adaptation.

### 1.2 Why This Framework Matters
Human interaction is not reduced to physical contact or simple proximity.
It emerges from shared attention, communicative intent, social roles, mutual adaptation, and contextual expectations.
A robust interaction architecture must therefore integrate social cognition, interpersonal communication, group dynamics, relationship modeling, multimodal perception, and temporal reasoning.

### 1.3 Architectural Principles
* Interaction inference must be evidence-based and socially grounded.
* Interaction inference must preserve uncertainty when evidence is ambiguous, partial, or socially layered.
* Interaction inference must connect micro-behaviors to interaction patterns, relationships, shared activities, and shared intent.
* Interaction inference must distinguish cooperation from coincidence, teaching from performance, greeting from conflict, and helping from restraint.
* Interaction inference must support explanation, review, and socially responsible policy reasoning.

# 2. Human Interaction Foundations

Human interaction intelligence is grounded in a broad interdisciplinary foundation that spans social cognition, interpersonal communication, group behavior, interaction psychology, social perception, anthropology, and multimodal reasoning.

### 2.1 Social Cognition
* Social cognition explains how humans infer the minds, goals, and emotions of others.
* It includes theory of mind, perspective taking, shared attention, and social prediction.
* The observer does not merely detect bodies; the observer infers a social relation, an interaction state, and an evolving shared agenda.
* Social cognition enables the distinction between casual coexistence and meaningful engagement.

### 2.2 Interpersonal Communication
* Communication is the central vehicle of interaction.
* It includes speech, gesture, gaze, posture, timing, turn-taking, silences, and prosody.
* Communication can be explicit or implicit, verbal or nonverbal, cooperative or antagonistic.
* A meaningful interaction is often a conversation, whether spoken or embodied.

### 2.3 Group Behavior
* Group behavior describes how multiple people coordinate action, share attention, and maintain social norms.
* Group interaction involves leadership, role assignment, formation, synchronization, and collective response.
* Group dynamics can be cooperative, competitive, ceremonial, political, or conflictual.
* The same visible gestures can mean very different things depending on whether the group is collaborative or adversarial.

### 2.4 Collaborative Behavior
* Collaboration is the formation of a joint activity with shared goals.
* It requires coordination, role awareness, distributed effort, and mutual responsiveness.
* Collaboration is not simply several people acting near each other; it is a structured social process of joint action.
* Shared activity emerges when participants align their actions around a common objective.

### 2.5 Interaction Psychology
* Interaction psychology focuses on the mechanisms by which people influence one another.
* It covers affiliation, persuasion, social support, competition, avoidance, and conflict escalation.
* The interpretation of interaction depends on whether participants are aligned, disengaged, dominant, subordinate, cooperative, or resistant.
* Psychological states modify the meaning of gestures, gaze, and proximity.

### 2.6 Social Perception
* Social perception concerns the detection and interpretation of social signals.
* It includes the reading of facial expression, body orientation, interpersonal distance, gaze coordination, and habitual interaction style.
* Social perception must be relational because the same signal can imply intimacy, deference, warning, or hostility depending on context.
* It is therefore a multimodal and contextual process rather than a single-channel coding problem.

# 5. Interaction Hierarchy

Human interaction can be modeled as a hierarchy that accumulates evidence from micro-level behavior to high-level social policy.

### 5.1 Micro Behaviors
* Small gestural, postural, and vocal acts such as nodding, pointing, pausing, and glancing.
* Evidence accumulation at this layer should be explicit, provenance-aware, and explainable.

### 5.1 Interaction Cues
* Structured signals such as turn-taking, mutual gaze, shared attention, and object transfer.
* Evidence accumulation at this layer should be explicit, provenance-aware, and explainable.

### 5.1 Interaction Pattern
* A recurring or coherent exchange such as briefing, teaching, negotiating, or greeting.

### 5.1 Relationship
* The social relation that shapes the expected form and tone of the exchange.

### 5.1 Shared Activity
* A jointly pursued task such as cooking, rehearsing, diagnosing, or building.

### 5.1 Shared Intent
* The common social or task objective that motivates the interaction.

### 5.1 Policy
* The downstream application of the inferred interaction to safety, access, moderation, or support decisions.

### 5.2 Evidence Flow Across the Hierarchy
* Micro behaviors provide the signal substrate for interaction reasoning.
* Interaction cues turn local motions into social signals.
* Interaction patterns assemble cues into recognizable exchanges.
* Relationship forms the social frame that contextualizes interpretation.
* Shared activity binds the interaction to a contextual task.
* Shared intent names the common goal driving the interaction.
* Policy translates this reasoning into action.

# 7. Interaction Confidence Architecture

Confidence is the degree of trust assigned to an inferred interaction.
It must be modeled as a structured profile rather than a single scalar.

### 7.1 Interaction confidence
* The trust in the inferred interaction type.
* Confidence increases when evidence is consistent, temporally sustained, relationally coherent, and socially plausible.

### 7.1 Relationship confidence
* The trust in the inferred relationship structure.
* Confidence increases when evidence is consistent, temporally sustained, relationally coherent, and socially plausible.

### 7.1 Contextual confidence
* The trust in the social and environmental support for the interpretation.

### 7.1 Temporal confidence
* The trust in the persistence and development of the interaction over time.

### 7.1 Multimodal confidence
* The trust in the evidence gathered from several modalities.

### 7.2 Confidence Propagation
* Micro-behavior confidence feeds interaction cue confidence.
* Cue confidence supports pattern confidence.
* Pattern confidence and relationship confidence jointly support shared activity inference.
* Shared activity and role confidence contribute to shared intent confidence.
* Policy decisions should reflect the full confidence profile rather than a single number.

# 8. Interaction Uncertainty Architecture

Uncertainty is essential because social scenes often contain ambiguous roles, partial cues, overlapping relationships, and culturally variable norms.

### 8.1 Interaction ambiguity
* The observed exchange could fit multiple social interpretations.
* The system should preserve this uncertainty explicitly rather than forcing a premature social label.

### 8.1 Relationship uncertainty
* The relationship between participants is not clear.
* The system should preserve this uncertainty explicitly rather than forcing a premature social label.

### 8.1 Social uncertainty
* The social meaning of a gesture or interaction is contested.

### 8.1 Contextual uncertainty
* The setting does not clearly support one interpretation over another.

### 8.1 Temporal uncertainty
* The interaction is too brief or unstable to infer a stable pattern.

### 8.2 Uncertainty Propagation
* Ambiguous gestures raise uncertainty at the micro-behavior level.
* Weak turn-taking or inconsistent gaze raises uncertainty at the interaction-cue level.
* Conflicting role cues raise uncertainty about relationship and shared activity.
* High uncertainty should trigger conservative handling and observation.

# 9. Temporal Interaction Intelligence

Interaction unfolds over time.
A single frame can be misleading, while a sequence reveals initiation, engagement, cooperation, interruption, escalation, de-escalation, and termination.

### 9.1 Interaction initiation
* The interaction begins through greeting, approach, request, or joint attention.
* Temporal continuity is essential for distinguishing a genuine social exchange from a coincidental overlap of behaviors.

### 9.1 Engagement
* Participants become mutually responsive and maintain a shared focus.
* Temporal continuity is essential for distinguishing a genuine social exchange from a coincidental overlap of behaviors.

### 9.1 Cooperation
* The interaction stabilizes around a shared task or social objective.

### 9.1 Interruption
* The exchange is disrupted by a new participant, a new topic, or external demand.

### 9.1 Escalation
* The interaction becomes more intense, more conflictual, or more urgent.

### 9.1 De-escalation
* The interaction relaxes, resolves, or returns to a neutral state.

### 9.1 Termination
* The interaction closes through disengagement, mutual acknowledgment, or handoff.
