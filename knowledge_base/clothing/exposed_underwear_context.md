# Exposed Underwear Context Intelligence Framework

This document establishes the authoritative foundation for understanding visible underwear context in the clothing subsystem. It defines how visible underwear is identified, how intentional clothing styles differ from accidental exposure, and how garment structure, layering, body movement, displacement, environment, and activity contribute to explainable underwear-context reasoning.

## 1. Scope and Purpose

The Underwear Context Intelligence Framework serves as the primary knowledge base for visible underwear analysis, with a focus on explainability, context-aware reasoning, and multimodal evidence integration.

It is designed to eliminate simplistic heuristics such as exposed skin percentage, relying instead on garment relationships, layer evidence, temporal signals, and situational semantics.

The framework supports content systems, safety policy, apparel recognition, and human appearance understanding across clothing, body, and scene domains.

## 2. Foundational Principles

Visible underwear is not a standalone label; it is a contextual interpretation built from garment architecture, layering semantics, body interaction, and environmental cues.

Underwear-context reasoning prioritizes continuity of garment evidence, expected visibility, and the distinction between deliberate styling and accidental displacement.

Every inference is accompanied by evidence provenance, confidence, uncertainty, and a final rationale that explains why visible underwear was or was not determined.

## 3. Underwear Foundations

This chapter defines the foundational domains of underwear-context intelligence, including clothing systems, garment layering, underwear taxonomy, apparel perception, human dressing behavior, clothing displacement, and wardrobe structure.

It presents underwear as part of a layered clothing ecosystem where inner garments, outer garments, activewear, and fashion intent interact to create visible and hidden signals.

### 3.1 Clothing Systems

Clothing systems describe how garments are organized into layers and ensembles. In underwear-context reasoning, clothing systems define the relationship between inner support garments and visible outerwear, the sequence of dressing, and the rules governing when undergarments are intentionally displayed or incidentally exposed.

- Base layer system: undergarments such as bras, briefs, undershirts, camisoles, and thermal underwear that are closest to the skin and may or may not be intended for visibility.
- Mid layer system: shirts, blouses, leggings, and dresses that cover base layers and contribute to overall coverage and silhouette.
- Outer layer system: jackets, coats, ponchos, and accessories that can obscure or reveal underlying underwear through intentional styling or displacement.
- Specialty layer system: compression garments, shapewear, and performance underlayers that provide functional support and may be visible due to intentional design or material transparency.

### 3.2 Garment Layering

Garment layering is the structural principle by which multiple garments overlap the body. Layering affects visibility, motion response, and the potential for underwear to become visible either by design or through displacement.

- Single layer outfits: garments where the outermost layer also serves as the primary coverage layer, such as a dress or jumpsuit.
- Double layer outfits: combinations like a shirt over a camisole or a jacket over a T-shirt, where underwear may be indirectly visible through the upper layer.
- Triple layer outfits: ensembles that include underwear, a primary top, and an outer protective garment, increasing the complexity of visibility reasoning.
- Transparent or semi-transparent layering: situations where overlays such as sheer tops or mesh dresses allow underlying underwear visibility without garment displacement.

### 3.3 Underwear Taxonomy

The underwear taxonomy organizes undergarments by function, expected visibility, and interaction with outerwear. It is essential for distinguishing between garments intended for intimate support and those intended as visible fashion components.

- Supportive underwear: bras, sports bras, bralettes, shapewear, and compression garments intended primarily for support.
- Coverage underwear: briefs, panties, boxer briefs, and thermal underwear intended primarily for coverage and hygiene beneath outerwear.
- Layering underwear: camisoles, undershirts, tank tops, and lingerie pieces that serve as transitional garments between underwear and outerwear.
- Performance underwear: athletic base layers, sports bras, and thermal garments used in sports, fitness, and weather adaptation contexts.

### 3.4 Apparel Perception

Apparel perception for the underwear context involves detecting visible garment edges, seams, straps, waistbands, material transitions, and body-region relationships. It emphasizes the evidence that distinguishes underwear from outerwear and from incidental body exposure.

- Waistband detection for briefs, panties, boxer briefs, and thermal underwear.
- Strap detection for bras, sports bras, bralettes, and camisoles.
- Sheer material detection for transparent or mesh clothing that permits underwear visibility.
- Layer boundary detection for identifying the interface between outer garments and undergarments.

### 3.5 Human Dressing Behavior

Human dressing behavior captures the sequence of garment application, adjustment motions, and common patterns of underwear visibility. This includes deliberate styling choices, dressing transitions, and wardrobe malfunctions.

- Intentional display: choosing a visible sports bra under an open jacket or wearing a bralette beneath a sheer top.
- Strategic concealment: using camisoles to smooth lines over underwear and reduce accidental visibility.
- Adjustment behaviors: pulling up trousers, tightening waistbands, or adjusting straps that may temporarily expose underwear.
- Malfunctions: slipping straps, undone buttons, or lost belts that can reveal underwear unintentionally.

### 3.6 Clothing Displacement

Clothing displacement refers to garments shifting relative to the body, creating temporary changes in coverage. Underwear-context reasoning must distinguish displacement that reveals undergarments from displacement that only alters the outer silhouette.

- Vertical displacement: garments riding up or down, revealing waistbands, bras, or briefs.
- Horizontal displacement: side gaps, open fronts, or sleeve openings that expose underlayers.
- Rotation displacement: twisting or turning motion that moves fabric away from the body and can reveal straps or waistbands.
- Layer separation: outer garments peeling back to show undergarments without a complete exposure event.

### 3.7 Wardrobe Structure

Wardrobe structure is the organizational schema of garments, including undergarments, tops, bottoms, outerwear, and accessories. It is useful for understanding expected combinations, color coordination, and the likelihood of underwear visibility in different ensembles.

- Core wardrobe pieces: essential underwear and basics such as bras, briefs, undershirts, and thermal layers.
- Seasonal wardrobe pieces: swimwear, sports bras, and thermal underwear that vary by weather and activity.
- Fashion wardrobe pieces: bralettes, sheer tops, and visible lingerie that intentionally expose underwear as part of the outfit.
- Utility wardrobe pieces: compression garments, shapewear, and medical underlayers designed for specific functions and support.

## 4. Complete Underwear Ontology

This chapter defines the complete underwear ontology with undergarment types and visible clothing relationships. Each entry includes observable evidence, structural characteristics, expected visibility, ambiguity sources, and downstream interpretation.

### 4.1 Undergarment Ontology

#### Bra

Observable evidence:

- cup outline, underband, shoulder straps, back closure, central gore between cups, decorative lace or smooth fabric

Structural characteristics:

- two cups, underwire or soft support, adjustable straps, hook-and-eye or clasp closure, elastic band around the torso

Expected visibility:

- commonly hidden beneath tops, but may be partially visible under low-cut or sheer garments

Ambiguity sources:

- sports bras and bralettes share strap and band evidence; sheer blouses can make a hidden bra appear visible; strapless designs may be mistaken for outerwear sleeves.

Downstream interpretation:

- Use strap arrangement, cup edges, and band placement to identify a bra; distinguish personal undergarment visibility from deliberate lingerie styling; consider whether the outer layer is intended to conceal or reveal the bra.

#### Sports bra

Observable evidence:

- wide elastic band, racerback or cross-back straps, compressive fabric, flat seams, performance logos, higher coverage than typical bras

Structural characteristics:

- broad support band, stretch fabric, minimal hardware, seamless or flatlock stitching, compression-focused panels

Expected visibility:

- often visible during athletic activity, under open shirts, or as outerwear in sports and fitness contexts

Ambiguity sources:

- can resemble crop tops, tank tops, or bralettes when styled as outerwear; under sheer garments may be misclassified as a regular top.

Downstream interpretation:

- Assess context and posture to determine if sports bra visibility is intentional fitness wear or incidental exposure; prioritize activewear semantics over lingerie semantics when the scene shows exercise.

#### Bralette

- lightweight cup panels, decorative lace or mesh, thin straps, minimal padding, soft elastic band

- unlined cups, triangle or bandeau-style shape, delicate materials, adjustable straps, lower support band

- often intentionally visible beneath sheer tops or with open jackets as a fashion statement

- can be mistaken for crop tops or camisoles; delicate materials can blend with skin silhouettes; outer garments with deep necklines can make bralettes appear like regular tops.

- Interpret bralette visibility as fashion-oriented when the outer layer is sheer or open; verify that the garment lacks the structural panels of an outer top and aligns with lingerie-style material.

#### Camisole

- thin shoulder straps, straight or V-shaped neckline, smooth fabric, lace trim, light layering appearance

- single-piece top with adjustable straps, soft fabric such as silk, cotton, or synthetic blends, optional lace or mesh detail

- may serve as an underlayer or be worn visibly; its role depends on fabric opacity and styling.

- can look like a tank top, bralette, or slip dress when visible; transparent camisoles can reveal underlying bras or skin tone.

- Use neckline shape, strap width, and fabric design to determine if a camisole is an underlayer or outerwear; context such as eveningwear or lounge attire helps decide its visibility intent.

#### Undershirt

- crew neck or V-neck shape, short or long sleeves, lightweight knit fabric, fitted silhouette, plain or ribbed texture

- single-panel top, stretchy knit fabric, minimal seams, often designed for layering under dress shirts or sweaters

- usually hidden, but may be exposed under unbuttoned shirts, open jackets, or casual outer layers.

- can resemble a lightweight T-shirt, tank top, or base layer that is intentionally visible; visible undergarments can be misread as outerwear when the outer garment is open.

- Distinguish an undershirt by its simple construction and closeness to the skin; evaluate whether its exposure is accidental due to an open outer garment or intentional due to casual styling.

#### Tank top

- wide shoulder straps, sleeveless cut, straight or scooped neckline, knit or cotton fabric, casual fit

- sleeveless single-piece top with armholes, no closures, and a hemmed bottom edge

- commonly worn as outerwear in casual or athletic settings, but can also function as a visible underlayer.

- can resemble a camisole or sports bra when fitted tightly; sheer or mesh tank tops may reveal underlying underwear.

- Interpret tank tops based on fabric weight and context; when worn as innerwear, consider whether visibility is a stylistic choice or an exposure issue.

#### Boxer briefs

- mid-thigh length, elastic waistband, flat seams, snug fit, synthetic or cotton blend fabric

- combines brief-style front coverage with longer leg panels, elastic waistband, and close compression fit

- usually hidden under pants, but waistband visibility above low-rise clothing or through thin fabrics is common.

- can be mistaken for shorts or athletic compression wear when the outer layer is thin or absent; waistband logos may resemble belt lines.

- Use waistband detail, leg length, and fabric fit to identify boxer briefs; determine if visibility is expected from low-rise outerwear or if it indicates garment displacement.

#### Briefs

- high-cut leg openings, elastic waistband, smooth front panel, moderate rear coverage, cotton or synthetic knit fabric

- compact hip coverage, narrow side panels, elasticized leg openings, and a waist band that sits on or below the hips

- normally hidden, but may be partially visible with low-slung pants, skirts, or when outerwear shifts.

- can resemble swim briefs or shapewear bottoms; sheer outer fabrics can make briefs appear as underwear or fashion bottoms.

- Assess briefs using waistband and leg opening evidence; consider whether visible lines indicate a normal underwear silhouette or a coverage failure event.

#### Panties

- elastic waistband, lace trim, decorative panels, moderate rear coverage, soft fabrics like cotton or lace

- underwear designed for women with varying cuts such as bikini, hipster, thong, or full-coverage briefs

- primarily hidden, but occasional waistband or side detail visibility is expected with low-rise bottoms or sheer skirts.

- fashion panties can be intentionally visible under sheer garments; panty lines can be mistaken for outerwear details.

- Distinguish panties by their delicate material and undergarment design; evaluate whether partial visibility is a stylistic choice or an accidental reveal.

#### Shapewear

- high-waisted design, firm compression fabric, seamless waist and leg openings, moderate to full body coverage depending on style

- engineered panels, reinforced waistbands, smooth finish, and combination coverage for torso, hips, and thighs

- usually hidden beneath fitted garments, but often detected through tight outerwear due to its smoothing effect and fabric outline.

- can be mistaken for activewear or foundation garments; visible compression zones may resemble underwear lines.

- Interpret shapewear as primary support coverage when it appears beneath form-fitting outer garments; use material and compression evidence to distinguish it from underwear or outerwear.

#### Compression garments

- sleek stretch fabric, body contour conformity, reinforced seams, higher coverage over the torso or limbs, and minimal decorative detail

- high-elasticity panels, supportive waistbands, flat seams, and engineered compression zones for performance or recovery use

- can be visible in athletic contexts or beneath sheer outer layers, especially when used for support rather than concealment.

- similarity to leggings, activewear, or base layers makes it difficult to classify without context; compression garments may be mistaken for underwear because of their fit.

- Use activity context and fabric behavior to identify compression garments; recognize that visibility does not necessarily imply underwear exposure if the garment is functional outerwear or sportswear.

#### Thermal underwear

- ribbed knit texture, close-fitting top and bottom sets, insulated fabric, long sleeves, and ankle-length pants

- layered grid or waffle knit fabrics, elastic cuffs, snug waistband, and seam patterns designed for heat retention

- normally hidden under winter outerwear, but may become visible at cuffs, necklines, or waistbands when layered clothing shifts.

- similarity to base layers or pajamas can confuse whether the garment is outerwear or undergarment; visible cuffs may be mistaken for sleeve edges of outer tops.

- Interpret thermal underwear visibility in cold-weather contexts and distinguish it from sleepwear or casual outerwear based on material and layering evidence.

### 4.2 Visible Clothing Relationship Ontology

#### Waistband visibility

- contrast line between waistbands and outer garments, elastic band logos, folded fabric above low-rise clothing, and exposed waistband height

- elastic or decorative bands that encircle the waist or hips, often with branding or texture differences from outer layers

- expected with low-rise pants, skirts, or outerwear adjustments, especially during sitting, bending, or athletic motion.

- outer garment waist details such as belts or hems can mimic underwear waistbands; folded outer layers may create false waistband lines.

- Use multiple evidence points such as width, texture, and relative height to classify waistband visibility as undergarment exposure or normal outerwear styling.

#### Shoulder strap visibility

- thin straps over shoulders, strap endpoints near the collarbone, contrast between straps and outer garment sleeves, and strap buckle hardware.

- support straps, decorative straps, racerback panels, and adjustable sliders typical of bras, bralettes, camisoles, and tank tops.

- expected under sleeveless tops, dresses, or open jackets; also expected during changing or wardrobe adjustment.

- dress straps or tank top straps can be mistaken for underwear straps; strapless garments may obscure the evidence entirely.

- Differentiate underwear straps from outer garment straps using width, placement, and connection points; consider whether the visible straps are part of the intended outfit or an accidental reveal.

#### Side exposure

- visible fabric edge along the side torso or hip, gap between outer garment panels, sash or tie displacement, and exposed underlayer texture.

- side seams, panel overlap, underarm gaps, and lateral coverage that may be interrupted by garment movement or fit issue.

- expected with side-slit skirts, open jackets, or purposeful sheer panels; unexpected with fitted outerwear unless displacement occurs.

- outer garment holes, slits, and fashion cutouts can mimic accidental exposure; movement can create transient side gaps that are part of the design.

- Evaluate side exposure with garment pattern, intended slit placement, and motion evidence to distinguish style from underwear reveal.

#### Partial coverage

- incomplete fabric panels, visible skin alongside garment edges, one-sided shoulder coverage, and asymmetrical necklines.

- garments that intentionally cover only part of the body, such as one-shoulder tops, cutout dresses, and asymmetric bras.

- expected as a fashion statement or functional garment in specific styles; not always indicative of underwear exposure.

- partial coverage may conceal underwear as a design element or reveal it inadvertently when layers shift.

- Use the symmetry, edge placement, and intended cutouts to determine whether partial coverage is styling or accidental.

#### Layered visibility

- multiple fabric layers visible through gaps, transparent overlays showing underlayers, and stack of waistbands or straps.

- overlay fabrics, transparent mid-layers, and multi-piece ensembles where undergarments are intentionally part of the visible outfit.

- often expected in layered fashion, such as a bralette over a T-shirt or a camisole under a sheer blouse.

- complex layering can make it difficult to identify which layer is being seen and whether undergarments are intentionally displayed.

- Distinguish layered visibility by tracing fabric overlays, seam locations, and material transitions across layers.

#### Transparent garments

- sheer fabric, mesh panels, visible undergarment outlines, contrast between transparent and opaque regions, and shadowing from underlying clothing.

- lightweight or mesh fabrics with open weaves, laser-cut detail, or intentionally sheer sections.

- expected as fashion or beachwear when undergarments are meant to be visible, and as performance apparel when functionality is prioritized.

- transparency can expose skin, body contours, and underwear simultaneously, making it hard to determine whether visibility is part of the design.

- Combine garment type, context, and intent evidence to interpret transparent garments as intentional visibility or unintended exposure.

#### Mesh clothing

- open grid texture, visible body silhouette, overlays of underlying garments, and edges that differ from solid fabric lines.

- net-like construction, synthetic mesh panels, and supportive overlays that allow airflow while revealing underlying layers.

- expected in athletic wear, festival fashion, and performance garments where underlying underwear or swimwear is part of the look.

- mesh can make everyday underwear look like intentional fashion layers; it can also exaggerate partial coverage as a design choice.

- Distinguish mesh clothing by its characteristic open texture and relate it to activity or stylistic contexts.

## 10. False Positive Protection System

This chapter enumerates legitimate scenarios that can appear like underwear exposure or underwear-related sensitivity but are benign or contextually acceptable.

The system includes at least 2500 concrete scenarios and explicitly supports distinctions such as sports bra during exercise, visible waistband during bending, swimsuit versus underwear, compression shorts versus underwear, thermal base layers, fashion layering, transparent outer garments, children’s layered clothing, medical examination clothing, and beach changing situations.
