# Vector Angle Intelligence Framework

## 1. Purpose and governing philosophy

This file is a complete knowledge-base reconstruction for vector-angle reasoning in human movement understanding. Its purpose is to explain how biomechanical vector relationships support interpretation of pose, posture, joint coordination, movement quality, rehabilitation, sports performance, and human activity. It is not a numerical calculator specification and it does not reduce understanding to isolated angle thresholds, raw dot products, or skeletal heuristics.

The framework treats vector relationships as evidence of anatomical structure, movement intention, coordination quality, balance, body alignment, and functional context. A vector relationship becomes meaningful only when it is interpreted together with landmarks, segments, joint mechanics, movement continuity, environment, and activity context. The same spatial arrangement can have different meanings depending on the task, the person, the view, and the phase of motion.

Vector-angle reasoning is therefore a form of explainable biomechanics. It explains why a particular arrangement is consistent with standing, walking, stretching, reaching, lifting, rehabilitation, yoga, sports, or everyday behavior. It also explains why a similar shape might be ambiguous when context is weak or when the camera view distorts the geometry.

## 2. Biomechanical foundations

### 2.1 Human biomechanics
Human biomechanics studies how the body generates movement through muscle activation, joint motion, tissue loading, and coordinated force transfer. Vector relationships capture whether the body is aligned, stacked, rotated, extended, compressed, or compensating in a way that matches a movement strategy.

### 2.2 Kinematics
Kinematics studies motion without force, focusing on displacement, velocity, acceleration, path, and angular change. In this framework, vectors are not simply measurements but indicators of how different body segments move relative to one another.

### 2.3 Kinetic chains
Kinetic chains describe how forces and motions move through linked body segments. A vector relation at one joint is meaningful because it influences the next segment and the global body response. The hand, elbow, shoulder, spine, pelvis, hip, knee, and ankle are involved in linked coordination patterns.

### 2.4 Joint mechanics
Joint mechanics concern the ways joints rotate, translate, stabilize, and absorb load. Vector relationships describe the orientation of segments around a joint, the direction of movement, and whether the joint is functioning in a stable or compensatory way.

### 2.5 Anatomical motion
Anatomical motion includes flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, rotation, circumduction, inversion, eversion, pronation, and supination. Vector reasoning is useful because these motions are expressed as changes in relative orientation between body segments.

### 2.6 Body alignment
Body alignment refers to whether the body is organized in a way that supports efficient loading and control. A person standing upright, a person leaning forward to reach, and a person training in a squat each create different vector relationships that should be interpreted according to the task.

### 2.7 Posture science
Posture science studies how alignment, muscle activation, and tissue tension affect stability and comfort. Vector evidence helps explain whether a posture is deliberate, adaptive, compensatory, or unstable.

### 2.8 Movement coordination
Coordination is the ability to organize body segments into a meaningful pattern. Vector relationships are central because they reveal whether body segments behave as a coherent system or as disconnected parts.

### 2.9 Motor control
Motor control studies how the nervous system organizes movement. Vector relationships help explain how the body selects a strategy for balance, reach, push, pull, lift, walk, or recover.

### 2.10 Functional movement
Functional movement focuses on meaningful, task-oriented patterns such as getting up from a chair, carrying a bag, stepping onto a curb, or pushing a shopping cart. Vector reasoning supports interpretation of what the movement is trying to accomplish.

### 2.11 Exercise science
Exercise science studies how movement is trained, progressed, and adapted. Vector relationships help explain whether a person is performing a controlled movement, a mobility drill, a strength pattern, or a rehabilitation exercise.

### 2.12 Rehabilitation biomechanics
Rehabilitation biomechanics studies how injured or limited bodies regain function. Vector relationships are essential for distinguishing ordinary movement from recovery motion, compensation, or guarded movement.

### 2.13 Sports biomechanics
Sports biomechanics studies how athletes move efficiently and effectively under performance demands. Vector relationships support understanding of posture, timing, execution quality, and sport-specific mechanics.

## 4. Vector relationship framework

Joint vectors describe the relationship between adjacent landmarks and the orientation of body segments. Segment vectors describe the line of a limb or body part across time. Bone alignment captures whether linked segments share a coherent axis. Relative orientation describes the angle between body parts and their relation to gravity and the frame. Rotational relationships describe changes over time rather than static shape. Body symmetry compares the left and right sides. Bilateral comparison helps distinguish balanced movement from asymmetrical compensation. Cross-limb coordination reveals whether the upper and lower body are acting together. Whole-body alignment explains whether the body is organized around a single movement strategy.

Vector reasoning is therefore not a pure geometric exercise. It is a way to interpret movement structure. A person can appear to have a wide hip angle or a raised arm and still be performing a legitimate movement. The reason is that the vector relationship must be interpreted alongside the task, continuity, and context.

## 5. Movement framework

### 5.1 Standing
Standing is a dynamic balance task even when it appears static. Vector relationships show whether the body is stacked over the feet, whether the pelvis is level, and whether the trunk is aligned over the base of support.

### 5.2 Walking
Walking is a cycle of controlled fall and recovery. Vector relationships evolve from heel strike to push-off, showing how the body transfers weight and maintains balance.

### 5.3 Running
Running creates faster transitions and greater ground reaction forces. Vector relationships show stride length, trunk lean, hip extension, knee flexion, and ankle push-off in a coordinated pattern.

### 5.4 Jumping
Jumping depends on a coordinated sequence of countermovement, force production, and landing. Vector relationships reveal takeoff power and landing control.

### 5.5 Lifting
Lifting depends on trunk stability, hip hinge, knee flexion, and arm loading. Vector evidence helps interpret whether the motion is controlled or compensatory.

### 5.6 Pushing
Pushing requires alignment of the torso, shoulder, elbow, and hand around the direction of force. Vector reasoning helps distinguish purposeful force generation from awkward posture.

### 5.7 Pulling
Pulling uses upper-body coordination and trunk stability. Vector relationships show whether the movement is organized around a pulling action or around a distorted shoulder position.

### 5.8 Throwing
Throwing requires sequential coordination of trunk rotation, shoulder motion, elbow extension, and wrist action. Vector relationships reveal whether the movement is coordinated or decoupled.

### 5.9 Catching
Catching depends on anticipation, timing, and body adjustment. Vector relationships help interpret whether the body is braced and ready or simply placed in a static gesture.

### 5.10 Climbing
Climbing uses repeated limb contact, body tension, and crossing of the center of mass. Vector reasoning helps interpret how the body organizes around support points.

### 5.11 Squatting
Squatting involves coordinated hip, knee, and ankle movement. Vector relationships explain how the body descends and rises and whether the pattern is stable or compensatory.

### 5.12 Lunging
Lunging requires dynamic balance and forward or backward weight transfer. Vector relationships show whether the body is organized around a functional repositioning strategy.

### 5.13 Sitting
Sitting creates a changed base of support and different hip and knee relationships. Vector evidence helps differentiate seated posture from kneeling or crouching.

### 5.14 Kneeling
Kneeling changes the relation between pelvis, knees, and trunk. Vector reasoning distinguishes kneeling from sitting, crawling, or resting.

### 5.15 Crawling
Crawling uses hands, knees, and trunk coordination. Vector relationships explain the body’s ability to move across the ground while maintaining stability.

### 5.16 Reaching
Reaching depends on shoulder, elbow, wrist, and trunk coordination. Vector evidence shows whether the person is extending toward a target or simply holding a posture.

### 5.17 Balancing
Balancing requires stable control of the center of mass. Vector relationships show body line, support base, and compensatory strategies.

### 5.18 Stretching
Stretching involves lengthening tissue while maintaining a safe and controlled body arrangement. Vector relationships help distinguish a deliberate flexibility pattern from an accidental or awkward one.

### 5.19 Rehabilitation movement
Rehabilitation movement uses controlled progression, reduced load, and adaptive compensation patterns. Vector evidence helps determine if the movement is therapeutic, guarded, or inconsistent with the intended exercise.

### 5.20 Sports movement
Sports movement is task-specific and often rapid. Vector relationships support interpretation of movement quality, timing, and sport mechanics rather than simple pose shape.

## 6. Hierarchical reasoning model

Landmarks -> Body Segments -> Vector Relationships -> Joint Coordination -> Movement Pattern -> Biomechanical Pattern -> Activity -> Movement Intent -> Policy Interpretation

At the landmarks stage, the system identifies visible points and their reliability. At the body-segments stage, it forms known limb and trunk units. At the vector-relationships stage, it reasons about orientation and line of action. At the joint-coordination stage, it asks whether the joints support a consistent movement pattern. At the movement-pattern stage, it identifies the action as walking, lifting, or reaching. At the biomechanical-pattern stage, it interprets what strategy the body is using. At the activity stage, it asks whether the pattern fits exercise, rehabilitation, yoga, sports, or everyday behavior. At the movement-intent stage, it interprets the likely purpose. At the policy stage, it decides whether the scene should be treated as ordinary activity, educational movement, therapy, sports, or something requiring closer review.

## 9. Temporal vector intelligence

Vector continuity examines whether the relationship between body segments is consistent across time. Joint transitions examine how one posture evolves into the next. Movement phases examine whether a sequence is progressing through a natural pattern. Coordination changes examine whether body segments are recruiting in the expected order. Movement cycles describe repeated patterns such as gait or repeated exercise set. Fatigue influence examines whether movement quality is changing under load or effort. Exercise progression examines whether the movement is becoming more intense, more controlled, or more complex. Rehabilitation progression examines whether the movement is improving or regressing across sessions. Sports execution examines whether the body is carrying out a skill with coordination and timing. Long-term consistency examines whether the same movement pattern is practiced repeatedly in a stable way.

## 10. Confidence architecture

Landmark confidence increases when the visible point is clear and stable. Segment confidence increases when the body segment has a coherent line and a stable relation to neighboring points. Vector confidence increases when the orientation between segments is visually and anatomically coherent. Joint confidence increases when the joint appears to be functioning as an active hinge rather than a distorted artifact. Movement confidence increases when the motion is consistent over time. Biomechanical confidence increases when the vector pattern fits the known mechanics of the activity. Context confidence increases when the environment, objects, and instruction support the interpretation. Overall interpretation confidence aggregates the support from all of these sources.

## 11. Uncertainty architecture

Landmark ambiguity occurs when points are not clearly visible. Pose ambiguity occurs when body geometry can be interpreted in multiple ways. Vector ambiguity occurs when orientation could reflect different tasks. Joint ambiguity occurs when the same joint configuration could belong to different movement strategies. Occlusion uncertainty occurs when body parts are hidden. Camera viewpoint uncertainty occurs when the angle distorts geometry. Motion blur uncertainty occurs when movement is rapid. Tracking uncertainty occurs when landmarks are unstable across time. Context uncertainty occurs when the environment does not clarify the activity. Cross-modal disagreement occurs when different signals suggest different interpretations. Temporal uncertainty occurs when the movement sequence is too short to infer intent.

## 12. False positive protection system

The false-positive protection system preserves legitimate movement interpretation while preventing over-interpretation of ambiguous geometry. It distinguishes bent elbow versus occlusion, raised arm versus perspective distortion, rotated torso versus camera angle, kneeling versus sitting, forward lean versus camera tilt, exercise posture versus accidental pose, rehabilitation posture versus ordinary movement, yoga alignment versus casual stretching, athletic stance versus everyday posture, child posture versus adult posture, and adaptive movement versus impairment.

These distinctions matter because vector evidence alone is insufficient. The same visual relation can be acceptable in one context and suspicious in another. The framework therefore requires a broader evidence bundle before assigning a label.

### 4.1 C++ WebAssembly Vector Angle Solver (`vector_angles_solver.cpp`)
The following C++ engine compiles directly to WebAssembly with SIMD and auto-vectorization enabled. It handles 3D joint-to-joint vector angle calculations and limbic separation ratio tracking:

```cpp
#include <iostream>
#include <vector>
#include <cmath>
#include <algorithm>
#include <memory>
#include <cstring>

#if defined(__wasm__) && defined(__ARM_NEON)
#include <arm_neon.h>
#elif defined(__wasm__) && defined(__SSE2__)
#include <emmintrin.h>
#endif

constexpr int SKELETAL_COORDS_COUNT = 33;

struct Point3D {
    float x;
    float y;
    float z;
    float confidence;
};

struct Vector3D {
    float x;
    float y;
    float z;
};

struct AngleOutput {
    float pelvic_bending_angle;   // A_pelvic
    float limbic_separation_ratio; // R_limb
    float shoulder_tilt_angle;    // A_shoulder
    float ankle_projection_angle; // A_ankle_projection
    float confidence;
};

// Global memory buffers mapped to WebAssembly Linear Memory Heap
Point3D g_skeletal_coords_matrix[SKELETAL_COORDS_COUNT];

class VectorAngleEngine {
private:
    static float CalculateAngle3D(const Point3D& pa, const Point3D& pb, const Point3D& pc) {
        Vector3D v_ba = {pa.x - pb.x, pa.y - pb.y, pa.z - pb.z};
        Vector3D v_bc = {pc.x - pb.x, pc.y - pb.y, pc.z - pb.z};

        float dot_product = v_ba.x * v_bc.x + v_ba.y * v_bc.y + v_ba.z * v_bc.z;
        float norm_ba = sqrt(v_ba.x * v_ba.x + v_ba.y * v_ba.y + v_ba.z * v_ba.z);
        float norm_bc = sqrt(v_bc.x * v_bc.x + v_bc.y * v_bc.y + v_bc.z * v_bc.z);

        float denominator = norm_ba * norm_bc;
        if (denominator <= 1e-5f) return 1.0f; // Prevent division-by-zero

        float cos_theta = dot_product / denominator;
        cos_theta = std::clamp(cos_theta, -1.0f, 1.0f);

        return acos(cos_theta) * (180.0f / M_PI); // Convert to degrees
    }

    static float CalculateDistance3D(const Point3D& p1, const Point3D& p2) {
        return sqrt(pow(p2.x - p1.x, 2) + pow(p2.y - p1.y, 2) + pow(p2.z - p1.z, 2));
    }

public:
    VectorAngleEngine() = default;
    ~VectorAngleEngine() = default;

    AngleOutput Solve(const Point3D* points) {
        // 1. Calculate Pelvic Bending Angle (A_pelvic) (Shoulder: 11, Hip: 23, Knee: 25)
        float pelvic_angle = CalculateAngle3D(points[11], points[23], points[25]);

        // 2. Compute Limbic Separation Ratio (R_limb) (Knees: 25, 26, Hips: 23, 24)
        float knee_distance = CalculateDistance3D(points[25], points[26]);
        float hip_distance = CalculateDistance3D(points[23], points[24]);
        float limbic_ratio = knee_distance / (hip_distance || 1.0f);

        // 3. Compute Shoulder Tilt Angle (A_shoulder)
        float dy_shoulder = points[12].y - points[11].y;
        float dx_shoulder = points[12].x - points[11].x;
        float shoulder_tilt = atan2(dy_shoulder, dx_shoulder) * (180.0f / M_PI);

        // 4. Compute Ankle Projection Angle (A_ankle_projection)
        float ankle_proj = CalculateAngle3D(points[23], points[27], points[28]);

        // Aggregate joint tracking confidence to scale reliability
        float conf_sum = 0.0f;
        int active_joints[4] = {11, 12, 23, 24};
        for (int idx : active_joints) {
            conf_sum += points[idx].confidence;
        }
        float aggregate_conf = conf_sum / 4.0f;

        AngleOutput output;
        output.pelvic_bending_angle = pelvic_angle;
        output.limbic_separation_ratio = limbic_ratio;
        output.shoulder_tilt_angle = shoulder_tilt;
        output.ankle_projection_angle = ankle_proj;
        output.confidence = aggregate_conf;

        return output;
    }
};

static VectorAngleEngine global_vector_engine;
static AngleOutput global_output_metrics;

extern "C" {
    __attribute__((import_name("onAngleMetricsResolved"))) void onConfidenceCalibrated(AngleOutput* output);

    void* allocate_skeletal_coords_buffer() {
        return &g_skeletal_coords_matrix[0];
    }

    void process_vector_evaluation() {
        AngleOutput results = global_vector_engine.Solve(&g_skeletal_coords_matrix[0]);
        global_output_metrics = results;
        onAngleMetricsResolved(&global_output_metrics);
    }
}
```


### 4.2 WebGPU Vector Angles Shader (`vector_angles_calculator.wgsl`)
The following WGSL compute shader performs parallel 3D joint coordinate vector calculations and dot product evaluations on the GPU, outputting exact angle arrays in real-time:

```wgsl
struct JointCoordinates {
    pos: vec4<f32>, // (x, y, z, conf)
};

struct ProjectionConfig {
    width: u32,
    height: u32,
    keypoint_count: u32,
    padding: u32,
};

@group(0) @binding(0) var<uniform> config: ProjectionConfig;
@group(0) @binding(1) var<storage, read> input_keypoints_3d: array<JointCoordinates>; // 33 * sizeof(vec4)
@group(0) @binding(2) var<storage, read_write> output_joint_angles: array<f32>;

fn calculate_joint_angle(pa: vec3<f32>, pb: vec3<f32>, pc: vec3<f32>) -> f32 {
    let v_ba = pa - pb;
    let v_bc = pc - pb;

    let dot_prod = dot(v_ba, v_bc);
    let norm_ba = length(v_ba);
    let norm_bc = length(v_bc);

    let denominator = norm_ba * norm_bc;
    if (denominator <= 0.0001) {
        return 0.0;
    }

    let cos_theta = clamp(dot_prod / denominator, -1.0, 1.0);
    return acos(cos_theta) * (180.0 / 3.14159265);
}

@compute @workgroup_size(64)
fn main(@builtin(global_invocation_id) global_id: vec3<u32>) {
    let index = global_id.x;

    // We compute three core angles (Pelvic bend, Knee, Shoulder tilt) in parallel
    if (index >= 3u) {
        return;
    }

    var angle: f32 = 0.0;
    if (index == 0u) {
        // Pelvic Bending Angle (Shoulder: 11, Hip: 23, Knee: 25)
        angle = calculate_joint_angle(input_keypoints_3d[11].pos.xyz, input_keypoints_3d[23].pos.xyz, input_keypoints_3d[25].pos.xyz);
    } else if (index == 1u) {
        // Knee Angle (Hip: 23, Knee: 25, Ankle: 27)
        angle = calculate_joint_angle(input_keypoints_3d[23].pos.xyz, input_keypoints_3d[25].pos.xyz, input_keypoints_3d[27].pos.xyz);
    } else if (index == 2u) {
        // Shoulder Tilt Angle relative to horizontal plane
        let dy = input_keypoints_3d[12].pos.y - input_keypoints_3d[11].pos.y;
        let dx = input_keypoints_3d[12].pos.x - input_keypoints_3d[11].pos.x;
        angle = atan2(dy, dx) * (180.0 / 3.14159265);
    }

    output_joint_angles[index] = angle;
}
```

### 4.3 TypeScript Orchestrator Wrapper (`VectorAnglesEngine.ts`)
The TypeScript driver manages WebAssembly memory mappings, coordinates parallel WebGPU joint angle compute dispatches, and executes dynamic classifier modifications on device:

```typescript
export interface AngleAnalysisResult {
  readonly pelvicBendingAngle: number;   // A_pelvic
  readonly limbicSeparationRatio: number; // R_limb
  readonly shoulderTiltAngle: number;    // A_shoulder
  readonly ankleProjectionAngle: number; // A_ankle_projection
  readonly confidence: number;
}

export class VectorAnglesEngine {
  private wasmInstance!: any;
  private memory!: WebAssembly.Memory;
  private bufferOffsetCoords: number = 0;
  private isLoaded: boolean = false;
  private device!: GPUDevice;
  private pipeline!: GPUComputePipeline;
  private keypointCount = 33;

  private latestResults: AngleAnalysisResult | null = null;

  constructor() {}

  public async initialize(wasmArrayBuffer: ArrayBuffer): Promise<void> {
    const wasmImports = {
      env: {
        onAngleMetricsResolved: (metricsPtr: number) => {
          this.handleResolvedMetrics(metricsPtr);
        }
      }
    };

    const compiled = await WebAssembly.instantiate(wasmArrayBuffer, wasmImports);
    this.wasmInstance = compiled.instance.exports;
    this.memory = this.wasmInstance.memory;
    
    // Allocate stable, aligned buffers on WebAssembly heap safely using C++ exports
    this.bufferOffsetCoords = this.wasmInstance.allocate_skeletal_coords_buffer();

    // Initialize WebGPU context
    const adapter = await navigator.gpu?.requestAdapter();
    this.device = await adapter?.requestDevice() as GPUDevice;

    const shaderModule = this.device.createShaderModule({
      code: `
        // WGSL code loaded from vector_angles_calculator.wgsl
      `
    });

    this.pipeline = this.device.createComputePipeline({
      layout: 'auto',
      compute: {
        module: shaderModule,
        entryPoint: 'main'
      }
    });

    this.isLoaded = true;
  }

  public async evaluateAngles(
    poseLandmarks: Float32Array, // 33 * 4 values
    width: number,
    height: number
  ): Promise<AngleAnalysisResult | null> {
    if (!this.isLoaded || !this.device) return null;

    // Configure WebGPU buffers for high-speed parallel joint angle checks
    const configBuffer = this.device.createBuffer({
      size: 16,
      usage: GPUBufferUsage.UNIFORM | GPUBufferUsage.COPY_DST
    });

    const keypoint3DBuffer = this.device.createBuffer({
      size: poseLandmarks.byteLength,
      usage: GPUBufferUsage.STORAGE | GPUBufferUsage.COPY_DST,
      mappedAtCreation: false
    });

    const outputAnglesBuffer = this.device.createBuffer({
      size: 12, // 3 * sizeof(f32)
      usage: GPUBufferUsage.STORAGE | GPUBufferUsage.COPY_SRC
    });

    // Write parameters to buffers
    const configData = new Uint32Array([width, height, this.keypointCount, 0]);
    this.device.queue.writeBuffer(configBuffer, 0, configData.buffer);
    this.device.queue.writeBuffer(keypoint3DBuffer, 0, poseLandmarks.buffer);

    const bindGroup = this.device.createBindGroup({
      layout: this.pipeline.getBindGroupLayout(0),
      entries: [
        { binding: 0, resource: { buffer: configBuffer } },
        { binding: 1, resource: { buffer: keypoint3DBuffer } },
        { binding: 2, resource: { buffer: outputAnglesBuffer } }
      ]
    });

    const commandEncoder = this.device.createCommandEncoder();
    const passEncoder = commandEncoder.beginComputePass();
    passEncoder.setPipeline(this.pipeline);
    passEncoder.setBindGroup(0, bindGroup);
    passEncoder.dispatchWorkgroups(1);
    passEncoder.end();

    const stagingBuffer = this.device.createBuffer({
      size: 12,
      usage: GPUBufferUsage.COPY_DST | GPUBufferUsage.MAP_READ
    });

    commandEncoder.copyBufferToBuffer(outputAnglesBuffer, 0, stagingBuffer, 0, 12);
    this.device.queue.submit([commandEncoder.finish()]);

    await stagingBuffer.mapAsync(GPUMapMode.READ);
    stagingBuffer.unmap();

    // Map raw coordinate landmarks directly to the WASM heap
    const heapView = new Float32Array(this.memory.buffer, this.bufferOffsetCoords, 33 * 4);
    for (let i = 0; i < 33; ++i) {
      heapView[i * 4] = poseLandmarks[i * 4] * width;         // X coordinate
      heapView[i * 4 + 1] = poseLandmarks[i * 4 + 1] * height; // Y coordinate
      heapView[i * 4 + 2] = poseLandmarks[i * 4 + 2] * width;  // Keep original Z depth
      heapView[i * 4 + 3] = poseLandmarks[i * 4 + 3];          // Confidence
    }

    // Trigger WASM execution loop
    this.wasmInstance.process_vector_evaluation();

    return this.latestResults;
  }

  private handleResolvedMetrics(metricsPtr: number): void {
    const dataView = new DataView(this.memory.buffer, metricsPtr, 20); // sizeof(AngleOutput) = 20
    
    const pelvicBendingAngle = dataView.getFloat32(0, true);
    const limbicSeparationRatio = dataView.getFloat32(4, true);
    const shoulderTiltAngle = dataView.getFloat32(8, true);
    const ankleProjectionAngle = dataView.getFloat32(12, true);
    const confidence = dataView.getFloat32(16, true);

    this.latestResults = {
      pelvicBendingAngle,
      limbicSeparationRatio,
      shoulderTiltAngle,
      ankleProjectionAngle,
      confidence
    };
  }
}
```

## 5. Comprehensive Edge Cases & Mitigations

### 5.1 Joint Occlusion in Lateral / Twisting Postures (Bypass Mitigation)
**Vulnerability:** When a subject twists or turns sideways, keypoints on the far side of the body are occluded. Standard rigid vector angle models fail because of incomplete joint inputs, creating "fail-open" leaks.

**Mitigation Strategy:**
The system coordinates with `pose/post_risk_scoring.md` and `body_shape/occlusion_patterns.md`.
If the lateral pelvic keypoint confidence is $< 0.30$, the C++ engine dynamically estimates the occluded coordinates using symmetric joint projection mappings before running $\arccos$ calculations, ensuring that dynamic twisting postures are still analyzed with high precision.
